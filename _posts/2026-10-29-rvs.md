## Quick recap of [the forward process](/posts/2026/10/frw/)

One noisy step:

$$
q(x_t|x_{t-1}) := \mathcal{N}(x_t; \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1}, \beta_t I)
$$ 

Jump to an arbitrary time step $$t$$:

$$
q(x_t|x_0) := \mathcal{N}(x_t; \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0, (1-\bar\alpha_t)I)
$$

Complete forward process:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&q(x_1,\cdots,x_T|x_0) = q(x_{1:T} | x_0) = \\
&q(x_1 | x_0) q(x_2 | x_1, x_0) \cdots q(x_T | x_{T-1}, \cdots, x_0) = \\
&q(x_1 | x_0) q(x_2 | x_1) \cdots q(x_T | x_{T-1}) = \\
&\prod_{t=1}^T q(x_t | x_{t-1})
\end{aligned}
$$

where $$x_0$$ is the observed data, $$x_1,\cdots,x_{T-1}$$ form a sequence of latent noisy states, and $$x_T$$ follows the terminal distribution, chosen to be approximately standard Gaussian: $$x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$.

## One denoising step

The reverse process is the opposite of the forward process. It performs denoising to remove the noise from the data step by step:

$$x_T \rightarrow x_{T-1} \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow x_1 \rightarrow x_0$$

Let’s assume there is a probability density function $$p_\theta$$, called approximate posterior, that allows us to reverse the individual noising steps: $$x_{t} \rightarrow x_{t-1}$$.

While the forward process is defined by a conditional Gaussian distribution q(x_t \rvert x_{t-1}) with mean $$\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1}$$ and variance $$\beta_t I$$, the reverse process depends on the learnable parameters $$\theta$$, which are optimized during training to effectively remove the noise. We can define a single denoising step p_\theta(x_{t-1} \rvert x_t) as a Gaussian distribution. 

$$
p_\theta(x_{t-1} | x_t) := \mathcal{N}(x_{t-1}; \mu_\theta(x_t, t), \Sigma_\theta(x_t, t))
$$

The mean of this Gaussian distribution is $$\mu_\theta(x_t, t)$$ and its variance is $$\Sigma_\theta(x_t, t)$$. Both are functions parameterized by $$\theta$$ and depend on the input values of current noisy state $$x_t$$ and time step $$t$$.

## Complete reverse process

Similar to the forward process, the complete reverse process describes a trajectory containing all the intermediate denoised states $$x_T,\cdots,x_1$$ starting at $$x_T$$. We represent this trajectory using a joint distribution $$p_\theta(x_T,\cdots,x_0)$$. To determine this joint distribution, we use the chain rule of probability to decompose it into individual conditional distributions. We then assume a first-order Markov chain, in which each noising step depends only on the previous state.

$$
p_\theta(x_T,\cdots,x_0) = p_\theta(x_{0:T}) := p(x_T)\prod_{t=1}^T p_\theta(x_{t-1}|x_t)
$$

## How to optimize parameters $$\theta$$?

To find the best $$\theta$$ that explains the observed data $$x_0$$, we need to minimize the negative log-likelihood (or maximize the log-likelihood) computed by marginalizing over all possible intermediate noisy states $$x_1, \ldots, x_T$$:

$$
-logp_\theta(x_0) = -log\int p_\theta(x_{0:T})dx_{1:T}
$$

In other words, over all possible latent trajectories connecting $$x_T$$ to $$x_0$$. Of course, trajectories must follow a specific Markov factorization for both

complete forward process (red path): 

$$
q(x_{1:T} | x_0) := \prod_{t=1}^T q(x_t | x_{t-1}) \qquad q(x_t|x_{t-1}) := \mathcal{N}(x_t; \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1}, \beta_t I)
$$

and complete reverse process (blue path): 

$$
p_\theta(x_{0:T}) := p(x_T)\prod_{t=1}^T p_\theta(x_{t-1}|x_t) \qquad p_\theta(x_{t-1} | x_t) := \mathcal{N}(x_{t-1}; \mu_\theta(x_t, t), \Sigma_\theta(x_t, t))
$$

With many diffusion steps, this high-dimensional marginalization becomes intractable. The likelihood requires integrating over the high-dimensional continuous latent trajectory.

So, instead of directly maximizing the exact likelihood, we find and optimize a tractable lower bound on it, called the Evidence Lower Bound, or ELBO:

$$
logp_\theta(x_0) \geq \mathbb{E}_{q(x_{1:T} | x_0)}\left[log \frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T} | x_0)}\right]
$$
